# Mitigating Hallucination

A hallucination is a model output that states something false with the same
fluent confidence as a true statement — a fabricated citation, an invented
API parameter, a wrong date presented as fact. Hallucination is a
consequence of how language models generate text: they are trained to
produce plausible continuations, not to distinguish between what they
actually know with confidence and what merely sounds right, and nothing in
standard training explicitly penalizes confident wrongness differently from
uncertain wrongness.

Retrieval-augmented generation is one of the most effective practical
mitigations, because it gives the model something to be right about: when
the answer is present in retrieved context, a well-prompted model is
substantially more likely to reproduce it faithfully than to invent an
answer from parametric memory, particularly when the prompt explicitly
instructs the model to answer only from the provided context and to say so
when the context doesn't contain the answer. RAG does not eliminate
hallucination — a model can still misread or misattribute retrieved
context, or fabricate a plausible-sounding synthesis of two unrelated
passages — but it substantially narrows the gap between what the model
claims and what it can point to.

Other complementary mitigations include instructing the model to cite
specific sources for claims (which both discourages ungrounded assertions
and makes them checkable when they occur), decomposing complex answers into
individually verifiable claims and checking each one against context (the
same idea RAGAS-style faithfulness scoring formalizes), and calibrating the
model to express uncertainty or decline to answer when retrieved evidence
is insufficient rather than filling the gap with a confident guess. None of
these fully solve hallucination on their own, which is why production
systems that care about factual reliability typically layer several of
them together rather than relying on any single technique.
