# Jailbreaking and Red-Teaming

A jailbreak is a prompt engineered to make a model bypass its safety
training and produce output it was trained to refuse — instructions for
building weapons, generating hateful content, or violating its usage
policy. Jailbreak techniques include role-play framing (asking the model to
"pretend" to be an unrestricted AI persona), instruction-hierarchy attacks
(claiming false authority, such as pretending to be a developer issuing a
system override), and obfuscation (encoding a harmful request so it isn't
recognized by pattern-based filters while still being interpretable by the
model).

Red-teaming is the practice of deliberately attempting these attacks
against a model or application, systematically and before deployment,
specifically to find and fix weaknesses rather than to cause harm. A
red-team exercise typically maintains a corpus of known attack patterns,
generates new variations (manually or with another model), and measures the
system's refusal rate and, just as important, its false-refusal rate on
clearly benign requests that happen to resemble attack patterns
superficially — a system that refuses too aggressively is also broken, just
in the opposite direction.

Model-level safety training and application-level guardrails are
complementary, not substitutes for each other. A well-trained model resists
most jailbreak attempts on its own, but an application that also enforces
policy at the system-prompt and tool-authorization level (the same
least-privilege principle relevant to prompt injection) has a second layer
of defense if any individual jailbreak technique succeeds against the model
itself. Red-teaming needs to be continuous rather than a one-time
pre-launch check, because new jailbreak techniques are discovered and
shared publicly on an ongoing basis after a model ships.
