# Retrieval Evaluation Metrics

Retrieval quality is usually measured against a set of queries with known
relevant documents, using rank-aware metrics rather than plain accuracy,
because where a relevant document lands in the ranking matters as much as
whether it appears at all.

Recall@k asks a simple question: for what fraction of queries does at least
one relevant document appear in the top k results? It's easy to interpret
and is the right metric when a downstream system (like a RAG generator) only
looks at the top k passages anyway — recall@k directly measures whether the
generator will even see the answer.

Mean reciprocal rank (MRR) scores each query by 1/rank of the first relevant
result (0 if none appear), then averages across queries. MRR rewards
putting the relevant document earlier, not just somewhere in the top k,
which recall@k ignores.

Normalized discounted cumulative gain (NDCG) generalizes further: it
accounts for multiple relevant documents per query and lets relevance be
graded rather than binary, discounting a document's contribution
logarithmically by its rank position, then normalizing by the score of the
ideal ranking so the metric is comparable across queries with different
numbers of relevant documents.

In practice, recall@k is the metric most directly tied to end-to-end RAG
quality, MRR is useful when a single best answer matters, and NDCG is
preferred when relevance is graded rather than binary. Reporting all three
against a fixed, versioned query set — and gating changes in CI when a
metric regresses beyond a tolerance — is standard practice for keeping a
retrieval system from silently degrading as the corpus, chunking, or model
changes.
